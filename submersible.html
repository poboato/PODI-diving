Vessel Specifications EXPERIMENTAL

The Colossus is a 5-person deep-sea submersible measuring approximately 22 feet in length, with a hull constructed from what we call "Carbon-Infused Composite Material" — carbon fiber weave sourced from a discount automotive supplier, bonded with proprietary epoxy resin from Home Depot. "It's the same stuff they use on sports cars," Gary explains. "Sports cars go fast. The Colossus goes down. It's just a different direction." The hatch is bolted shut from the outside with 17 bolts. The hull has been "theoretically tested" using computer simulations that Kyle ran on his laptop while also streaming Netflix. The simulation crashed. Kyle took that as a good sign. "If the computer couldn't handle the pressure, how can the water?" he said. We did not follow up on what that meant.

Why The Colossus Is Different

Most submersibles take years to design, millions of dollars to build, and are certified by classification societies like DNV or Lloyds. That's a lot of paperwork. That's a lot of "rules." That's a lot of people saying "that won't work" and "this is dangerous." The Colossus was built in 14 months in a rented warehouse using parts sourced from wherever Gary could find them. "Innovation happens when you stop asking for permission," Gary says. "If I asked an engineer whether a $30 LogiPlay controller could pilot a submersible to 3,800 meters, the engineer would say no. So I didn't ask. You don't get to change the world by filling out forms." Gary has described traditional submersible engineering as a "stifling orthodoxy" run by "people who care too much about not imploding." His core philosophy: "If you're not breaking rules, you're not innovating. Every great invention in history broke rules. The airplane broke the rule of 'humans can't fly.' The Colossus breaks the rule of 'carbon fiber at crush depth will catastrophically delaminate after repeated stress cycles.' Same energy." When a journalist asked if he was worried about the sub imploding, Gary leaned into the microphone and said: "Not even the ocean could sink this sub." Kyle, standing next to him, quietly muttered "that's literally what they said about the Titanic." Gary's response: "And look how famous it is."

🔧 Maintenance Philosophy Between dives, the Colossus is inspected visually by whoever's available. "If it looks fine, it's fine," Skip says. The hull is tapped with a wrench in various locations — a solid sound means go, a hollow sound means "tap again." The controller's AA batteries are replaced every third dive regardless of charge. "Batteries are cheap. Surface-to-seabed communications are not." The O-rings on the viewport are lubricated with whatever grease is closest. The trolling motors are tested in a Kids' pool behind the warehouse. If they spin, they're good. "Maintenance is 90% confidence," Gary says. "The other 10% is also confidence."

How We Compare

Traditional submersible manufacturers follow something called "industry standards" — a set of guidelines developed over decades by engineers, scientists, and regulatory bodies. These standards require things like "third-party hull inspections," "redundant life support systems," "certified materials," and "not using a video game controller as the primary navigation interface." PODI considers this approach "safe but boring." The Colossus was developed under a different framework we call "Agile Submersible Development." The principles: move fast, test later, full-depth-certify never. "If NASA had waited for every system to be 100% reliable, they'd never have gone to the moon," Gary argues. (NASA had a 0.4% failure rate across 135 Space Shuttle missions. The Colossus has had a 0% mission rate across zero actual dives. Gary says these numbers are "basically the same.") For the record: unlike the original Titanic, The Colossus has sonar. If anyone spots an iceberg at 3,800 meters, we have bigger problems than the ones the movie addressed. "Iceberg, right ahead!" is not a phrase you'll hear in The Colossus. For geological reasons. Mostly.

Frequently Asked Questions

Is The Colossus safe?

+

"Safe" is a relative term. Safe compared to what? Safe compared to not being at 3,800 meters in an uncertified submersible controlled by a $30 video game controller that was also used to beat Elden Ring? No, not compared to that. But compared to other things — like wrestling a bear, or standing outside during a lightning storm holding a metal pole — The Colossus is statistically somewhere in the middle, probably. We haven't run the numbers. "Safe is a feeling," Gary says. "And I feel safe." He also believes carbon fiber delamination is "a skill issue," so interpret that as you will. Our official position is that risk is "an acceptable price for innovation" and that "people who want guarantees should probably book a cruise instead."

Wait — is the hatch really bolted from the outside?

+

Yes. There are 17 bolts. They are tightened with a wrench by the surface crew after you're inside. "It's not that we don't trust you to open it," Skip explains. "It's that opening the hatch at 3,800 meters would be catastrophic, and we don't want anyone getting curious." The bolts cannot be operated from the inside. This is, according to Gary, "a safety feature, not a safety hazard — perspective is everything." Kyle adds: "It's like a roller coaster lap bar. Once it's down, you're committed. That's part of the thrill."

Why did the whistleblower get fired?

David — that was his name, we think — raised concerns about the carbon fiber hull's ability to withstand repeated stress cycles at depth. His replacement, a mannequin named Bubbles, has raised zero concerns, which Gary considers "a massive improvement in team morale."
